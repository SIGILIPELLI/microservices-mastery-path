    Bounded Contexts & Domain-Driven Design Basics¶

    What was run¶

    No new service for this lesson — this one is about a design technique, applied directly to the monolith.py shop app from Lesson 1, whose actual code and data you already have running on localhost:8001.

    The same word, two different things¶

    Look at the monolith's two concepts: Customer and Order. Now imagine the business grows and adds a support team that also talks about "customers" — but their idea of a customer includes a support-ticket history, a satisfaction score, and a list of past complaints. The sales team's idea of a customer includes a lead source, a sales rep, and a pipeline stage. Both teams say "customer." Neither means the same thing.

    This is the core insight of domain-driven design's bounded context: a model is only consistent and only has one meaning within a boundary. Outside that boundary, the same word can and should mean something else. A bounded context is not a diagram box you draw first — it's the boundary around a linguistically consistent chunk of the domain that you discover by listening to how people in the business actually talk, and noticing where the same word splits into two different meanings or two different sets of rules.

    Finding the seams in the shop domain¶

    Applying this to the shop app honestly, with just two entities, there's really only one plausible context boundary worth naming yet: Ordering (orders, items, quantities) and Customer Identity (name, email, contact info). The useful exercise isn't to force a split on a two-entity toy app — it's to notice the question that tells you a boundary exists:

    • Does Ordering need the customer's email, or does it only need a customer_id and the fact that the ID is valid?
    • If support tickets were added, would "customer" there need name/email at all, or just enough to look the person up — and does that mean support is its own context with its own, much smaller, customer model?

    The test for a real bounded-context boundary: two parts of the domain can use the word "customer" and mean genuinely different things, validated by genuinely different rules, without ever needing to reconcile those meanings directly. If they'd actually break if their models diverged, they're the same context. If they can happily diverge forever, they're different contexts — and that's your service boundary candidate.

    How It Actually Works¶

    A bounded context maps to a microservice boundary candidate because of a specific DDD rule: inside one bounded context, there is exactly one model for each concept, and that model is internally consistent. Across a context boundary, models can and do diverge, and that divergence is intentional, not a bug to be fixed with a shared schema.

    This gives you a concrete decomposition heuristic that beats "one service per noun" (the subject of Lesson 3):

    1. List the core nouns in the domain (Customer, Order, Payment, Shipment, ...).
    2. For each noun, ask which teams or parts of the business use it, and whether their rules about it genuinely differ — not just their field names, their actual business rules (e.g., "a customer's email must be verified before checkout" vs. "a customer's email is just a free-text contact field for support").
    3. Group nouns and rules that must stay mutually consistent into one context. A Payment context that needs to know an Order's total and currency, and must never see a stale total, is tightly coupled to Ordering — maybe the same context, maybe a very chatty neighbor that needs the saga pattern from Level 2.
    4. Each resulting context is a candidate service boundary — not yet a guaranteed one; Lesson 3 covers when a context is also the right size for one deployable service versus several, or part of a shared one.

    A context map — a diagram of which bounded contexts exist and how they relate (shared model, translation layer, one context being upstream of another) — is the deliverable DDD recommends before any code. It costs nothing to draw and it is the single most common missing artifact in microservices projects that end up with chatty, tangled service boundaries (the "distributed monolith" anti-pattern covered in Level 4, Lesson 8).

    Common mistakes¶

    • Drawing context boundaries around database tables instead of around meaning and rules — two tables can belong to the same context, and one table's concept can legitimately need to be split across two contexts with different views of it.
    • Trying to find one universal Customer model that satisfies sales, support, and ordering simultaneously — this is exactly the shared-model trap DDD is designed to avoid; a translation layer between contexts is normal and healthy, not a failure.
    • Doing this exercise once, in a kickoff meeting, and never revisiting it — bounded contexts shift as a business evolves, and a context map should be a living document, not a one-time artifact.

    Exercise¶

    Take the shop domain and add a Shipping concern to it on paper only (no code yet): once an order is placed, it needs to be packed, labeled, and tracked until delivered. Write down: (1) what a Shipment means to the Shipping context versus what "shipping info" might mean if it stayed inside Ordering instead, and (2) one concrete business rule that would only make sense in one of the two contexts, not both. Keep this answer — Lesson 3 uses it directly to decide whether Shipping deserves its own service.
